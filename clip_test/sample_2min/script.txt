In 1941, the Soviet Union looked finished. When Germany launched Operation Barbarossa in June, three million German soldiers stormed across the Soviet border — the largest invasion in human history.

Within weeks, entire Soviet armies were surrounded. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers were captured, and thousands of tanks and aircraft were destroyed on the ground. By autumn, German panzers stood within sight of Moscow, and the world expected the Soviet Union to collapse.

But it didn't. And the reason starts with what Adolf Hitler got wrong.

The German high command planned for a short war. They believed the Red Army would shatter like glass, and that Joseph Stalin's government would crumble under the first blow. They counted on capturing Moscow before winter, and they packed no winter clothing for their troops.

Instead, the Soviet Union did something extraordinary. As the Germans advanced, more than fifteen hundred factories were packed onto trains and shipped thousands of kilometers east, beyond the reach of German bombers. In the Urals and Siberia, entire industrial cities rose from empty steppe in a matter of months — and the tanks rolling off those lines went straight to the front.

At the same time, the Red Army kept raising new divisions faster than the Germans could destroy them. Men who had never held a rifle were trained, armed, and thrown into the line.

Then came December. In temperatures of forty degrees below zero, fresh Siberian divisions under General Georgy Zhukov struck the exhausted German army outside Moscow. German engines froze solid. Soldiers wrapped their boots in newspaper. The blitzkrieg that had conquered France in six weeks ground to a halt in the snow.

By January, the Germans were retreating — their first major defeat of the war.

The Soviet Union survived 1941 not because of one brilliant decision, but because of scale: its land, its factories, its people, and a willingness to sacrifice everything to keep fighting. Hitler had planned for a sprint. He got a war of attrition he could never win.
